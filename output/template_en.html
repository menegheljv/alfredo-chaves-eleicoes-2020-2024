00 · About the title

We no longer live like our parents

The title is a reference to “Como Nossos Pais” (“Like Our Parents”), by Belchior (1976, immortalized in Elis Regina's voice), one of the most iconic songs in Brazilian popular music. It's about people who insist on living stuck in the past and fail to notice that the new always arrives, sooner or later. Here that theme takes on a literal meaning: the data in this study shows, precinct by precinct, year by year, twenty years of an old political guard that survived five straight elections before finally being replaced. A metamorphosis of power, and, within it, the real distance between my father's defeated candidacy in 2004 and my brother's win two decades later.

00.1 · Twenty years of data

Five losses, then a win

Before the 2020→2024 turnaround documented below, this same municipality carries a longer story. From 2004 to 2020, the group behind this candidacy lost five consecutive mayoral elections in Alfredo Chaves, Brazil. In 2004 the candidate was Jorge Gabriel Meneghel, my father. The family and political lineage runs through every cycle since, up to my brother Hugo Luiz in 2024. The chart below, built the same way as everything else in this study, from official TSE data by voting precinct across all six elections, shows the vote share closing the gap, cycle by cycle, until 2024.

Full disclosure. Jorge Gabriel Meneghel, the 2004 candidate, is my father. Hugo Luiz, who won in 2024, is my brother. This project started as campaign work. The data pipeline came after, to understand what actually moved the result.
01 · Context

Two elections, same political base

Between 2020 and 2024 I worked as campaign director in two consecutive municipal elections in Alfredo Chaves, Brazil, for the majoritarian mayoral candidacy. In 2020, the ticket lost the mayoral race, winning just 1 of the 36 voting precincts that existed at the time — but Hugo Luiz Picoli Meneghel, today's mayor, had already been elected to the City Council in that same election, part of a bloc holding only 3 of 9 seats: a clear minority. In 2024, also coordinating the campaigns of the coalition's other council candidates, the same political base won all 42 voting precincts in the municipality, electing Hugo Luiz the youngest mayor in the history of Espírito Santo state — and expanding the bloc from 3 to 5 of the 9 seats on the Council, flipping it from minority to majority.

This document reconstructs that turnaround entirely from open, official TSE data — without relying on memory, internal campaign spreadsheets, or any non-public record — as an exercise in technical analysis over a real electoral dataset.

02 · Data source

TSE's Open Electoral Data Repository

Every number in this study comes from four extracts of the TSE Open Data Portal (Brazil's Superior Electoral Court), filtered to the municipality of Alfredo Chaves, Espírito Santo (TSE code 56057), electoral zone 012:

  • votacao_secao_2020/2024_ES.csv — votes by voting precinct (1,710 and 2,089 rows after the municipal filter)
  • votacao_candidato_munzona_2020/2024_ES.csv — consolidated result per candidate (72 and 73 records)
  • votacao_partido_munzona_2020/2024_ES.csv — party-line vote totals (9 and 11 records)
  • detalhe_votacao_munzona_2020/2024_ES.csv — turnout, abstention, blank/null votes
  • prestacao_de_contas_eleitorais_candidatos_2020/2024.csv (revenue and paid expenses) — extracted via HTTP range requests from a ~1.3 GB national file, without downloading the whole thing
  • perfil_eleitor_secao_2020/2024_ES.csv — electorate profile by precinct (gender, age bracket, education)
Transparency note. The continuity between the two majoritarian candidacies (2020→2024) is reconstructed from verifiable coincidences in the official data — the same local party base (Republicanos/PTB) and the fact that the 2024 winner, Hugo Luiz Picoli Meneghel, appears in the 2020 data as a city council candidate elected on the coalition that lost the mayoral race that year. No number was estimated, projected, or invented: where a figure wasn't publicly available, it was left out rather than approximated.

def clean_secao(df, year):
    keep = ['NR_ZONA', 'NR_SECAO', 'CD_CARGO', 'DS_CARGO',
            'NR_VOTAVEL', 'NM_VOTAVEL', 'QT_VOTOS']
    df = df[keep].copy()
    df['NR_SECAO']  = pd.to_numeric(df['NR_SECAO'], errors='coerce').astype('Int64')
    df['QT_VOTOS']  = pd.to_numeric(df['QT_VOTOS'], errors='coerce').fillna(0).astype(int)
    df['NM_VOTAVEL'] = df['NM_VOTAVEL'].str.strip().str.upper()
    df['ANO'] = year
    return df.dropna(subset=['NR_SECAO'])

04 · Key findings

From a broad defeat to a win in every precinct

In 2020, this political base's majoritarian candidacy won just 1 of 36 comparable precincts (precinct 9), taking 37.8% of the valid vote citywide (3,681 votes), against 53.4% for the winning candidate. In 2024, the same base — now under the coalition “Renovação e Juventude” (“Renewal and Youth”; Republicanos / PP / MDB / PSDB‑Cidadania federation) — won all 42 precincts in the municipality, with 56.4% of the valid vote (5,779 votes).

Of the 36 comparable precincts, 28 moved from a minority (<50%) to an absolute majority (≥50%) of the valid vote between the two elections — a swing averaging 20.9 percentage points in those precincts.

A different party every election

The consulta_cand data shows the group's candidate never ran under the same party twice in twenty years: PT in 2004, PSDB in 2008, PSD in 2012, PMDB in 2016, Republicanos in 2020, PP in 2024. The ideological spectrum shifted too, from a left-wing party in 2004 to center and right-wing parties from 2012 on, tracking the national realignment of Brazilian politics over the period — not an isolated local strategy choice.

Coalition size, though, doesn't explain the win on its own: in 2016, the candidate ran with the largest coalition of the whole series (9 parties) and still lost, with 48.0% of the vote. In 2020, with the smallest coalition (3 parties), he also lost. The 2024 coalition (5 parties: Republicanos, PP, MDB and the PSDB‑Cidadania federation) is mid-sized within the series — neither the largest nor the smallest.

On the opposing side, the pattern is the reverse. In the five elections this group lost, between 2004 and 2020, the winner was always PSB: Dr. Fernando took 2004, 2008, 2016 and 2020, and Roberto Fiorin, also PSB, took 2012 — four terms and two different names under the same party label, twenty years straight. That streak only breaks in 2024, when Rolmar Boteccia tries to keep PSB in power and loses to Hugo Luiz's PP. In other words: while the losing side changed parties every election, the winning side had a stable label for two decades — and it was precisely that stability that broke in 2024, along with the result.

05 · Legislative performance

The political flip in the City Council

Alfredo Chaves' City Council has 9 seats. In 2020, Hugo Luiz Picoli Meneghel's political base (then a council candidate for PTB, on the majoritarian coalition “Mudança com Transparência” — PTB / Republicanos / Patriota) elected only 3 council members, against 6 from opposing parties (the coalition that won the mayoral race: PP / PSB / PSDB, among others) — a clear minority. In 2024, with campaign coordination extended to every council candidate on the “Renovação e Juventude” coalition (Republicanos / PP / PSDB), the ticket's bloc grew to 5 of 9 seats: a simple majority, if a tight one.

06 · Financing and turnout

What it cost, and who showed up to vote

Data from the TSE's electoral campaign finance filings (revenue and expenses declared by every candidate, opponents included) and its turnout breakdown (attendance and abstention) round out the picture: the turnaround didn't come from a bigger electorate, and the revenue raised by the group's candidacy was never markedly higher than its competitors' — but the group's campaign converted that money into votes more efficiently than any other candidate for the office, in both elections.

Turnout doesn't explain the turnaround

The registered electorate grew just 4.6% (12,294 → 12,860) and turnout stayed practically flat — 79.15% in 2020, 79.74% in 2024. Since the ticket's candidate grew 57% in absolute votes (3,681 → 5,779) while the total electorate barely moved, the turnaround was predominantly conversion of voters who already voted, not mobilization of new ones.

Electorate profile: almost nothing changed

The electorate's demographic profile (perfil_eleitor_secao data, TSE) also stayed stable over time. The gender split has been slowly converging toward parity every election: 51.7% men / 48.2% women in 2008, 51.1% / 48.8% in 2012, 50.6% / 49.3% in 2016, 50.2% / 49.7% in 2020, and 49.9% / 50.0% in 2024 — the TSE never published this figure for 2004. Practically fifty-fifty all along, with a steady trend toward a slight female majority forming.

A data curiosity. The number of voters aged 16 to 24 was exactly 1,225 in 2020 and 1,225 in 2024 — same age bracket, same total, two different elections. A real coincidence, confirmed row by row in the official TSE database; it has no obvious causal explanation, but it rules out the idea that the turnaround was driven by a wave of newly-registered young voters — the municipality's young-voter base simply neither grew nor shrank between the two elections.

Cross-referencing with IBGE: the electorate grew, the population didn't

To understand whether the electorate's growth (9,247 registered in 2004 → 12,860 in 2024, +39%) reflects the municipality growing, we cross-referenced TSE data with IBGE's annual population estimates (SIDRA, table 6579, municipality code 3200300). The answer is no: Alfredo Chaves' estimated population stayed practically flat over the period (14,113 in 2004, ~14,373 in 2024), with dips in some years. The electorate didn't grow because the municipality grew — it grew because an increasing share of the population already living there became registered to vote.

Sex, race and income: do candidates look like the people who live here?

IBGE's 2022 Demographic Census (SIDRA, tables 9514 and 9605) makes it possible to go beyond total population and cross-reference sex and race/color with who actually runs for office. Alfredo Chaves' population is 49.3% women and 50.7% men, 61.4% white, 34.3% mixed-race (“pardo”) and 4.2% Black — numbers very close to the registered electorate's, which is also moving toward gender parity. Among candidates, though, the picture diverges: of the 75 mayoral and council candidates in 2020, 37.3% were women; in 2024, out of 76 candidates, that share fell to 31.6% — the opposite of the electorate's trend, which keeps moving closer to 50%.

A name behind the number. The Alfredo Chaves City Council's own legislative record (Commendation Motion No. 003/2024, authored by then-council-member and now-mayor Hugo Luiz) honors Regina Puppin as “the first and only Council member of the Municipality of Alfredo Chaves to hold a seat in the Municipal Legislature” — in other words, the only woman ever to hold a Council seat in the municipality's history, to this day. She doesn't appear among any of the council candidates the TSE registered between 2004 and 2024 consulted for this study, which indicates her term predates that period — the gap in female representation on the Council is older and deeper than the six elections analyzed here.

The racial composition of the candidacies follows a similar pattern. In 2020, white candidates were 61.3% of the total — almost identical to the municipality's population share of 61.4%. By 2024 that share rose to 69.7%, while mixed-race candidates fell from 29.3% to 22.4%: a drift away from Alfredo Chaves' racial composition, even as the share of Black candidates grew slightly (6.7% → 7.9%).

07 · Digital campaign

What the posts show about the race

This section steps outside the TSE dataset and uses a different source: the post history of the candidacy's official Instagram account (@hugoluiz11_), from the day of party affiliation through the election result. It isn't public, auditable data like the rest of this study — it's the campaign's own archive, reviewed post by post from screenshots exported from the app, with date, views, likes, comments and shares for each publication. We treat these numbers the way the campaign team saw them in real time: a measure of organic reach and engagement, not voting intention.

The timeline covers six milestones: affiliation to Progressistas (PP) on March 27, 2024; the pre-candidacy launch on June 7; the running-mate announcement for Cleber Bianchi, at the August 3 party convention; the public dispute over a suspended poll on September 22; the win on October 6; and the thank-you post for 5,779 votes, published hours after the result — the highest-engagement post of the entire campaign.

08 · Candidate profile

Who the candidates were, beyond the votes

consulta_cand and bem_candidato data (TSE) bring age, education, occupation and declared net worth for each candidate — public information, required by law at every election. The contrast with Hugo Luiz is the sharpest in the entire study.

Hugo Luiz was 25 on election day in 2024, holds a law degree (UFES) and, per the TSE's records, is white and single. The other two mayoral candidates that year were 61 and 71.

The 9 registered election polls

The TSE maintains its own system for registering election polls (pesqele‑divulgacao.tse.jus.br), required under Article 33 of Law 9,504/97. For Alfredo Chaves in 2024, 9 polls are on record, from 5 different firms, between April and September. We opened and read all 9 registration filings in PDF — and it's worth noting a real limit of the public data: the TSE doesn't publish the result (voting-intention percentage per candidate) in these filings, only formal confirmation of registration (firm, protocol, date, scope). It's the commissioning firm that releases the result, typically through a local press outlet.

We located the original document for 6 of the 9 polls — the full reports each institute produced for its client, with methodology, sample size and margin of error. Two of them (Apr 16 and Sep 21) had also been released to the press; the other four are real data, but restricted in circulation, never published by a press outlet:

  • Apr 15–16, 2024 — Instituto Solução (TSE ES‑09808/2024), 403 respondents, 4.9 p.p. margin, commissioned by AQUI NOTÍCIAS.COM (released).
  • Aug 29–31, 2024 — Instituto Veritá (TSE ES‑08806/2024), 352 respondents, 5.5 p.p. margin, commissioned by Marataízes Piúma Iriri Internet Ltda. (not publicly released).
  • Sep 17, 2024 — no institute or TSE registration number appears on the document (an atypical result — see the note below).
  • Sep 20–21, 2024 — Inove Consultoria (TSE ES‑04825/2024), 574 respondents, 4.0 p.p. margin, commissioned by H.A. Comunicações Ltda. (released by Montanhas Capixabas).
  • Sep 27–29, 2024 — Ipopes Pesquisa de Opinião Pública, 400 respondents (not publicly released).
  • Oct 1–2, 2024 — I9‑Inove Consultoria (TSE ES‑06358/2024), 373 respondents, 5.0 p.p. margin, commissioned by Portal ES Agora (not publicly released).
Data notes. One of the registered polls (Direta Propaganda e Eventos) was obtained, but the document referred to a different municipality (Conceição do Castelo, ES) — likely a filing error on the commissioning firm's side, so it wasn't used here. 2 of the 9 registered polls still have no document located. The full biographical profile of 2020 candidates required downloading an entire 85 MB national file (no partial-download support), which worked, but more slowly and less elegantly than the rest of the pipeline.

10 · Conclusion

What the data shows

Reading the results precinct by precinct confirms the 2020-to-2024 turnaround wasn't concentrated in a few strongholds: it was spread across nearly the entire electoral territory of the municipality, with an average gain of more than 20 percentage points in precincts where the majority flipped. That's consistent with a strategy of territorial reach rather than reinforcing already-favorable bases — the one precinct already won in 2020 (precinct 9) saw a relatively modest additional gain (+13.7 p.p.) compared with precincts that started from a loss.

The expanded campaign coordination in 2024 — including, for the first time, the council candidacies — coincides with a second turnaround, in the Legislature: the same political base that elected Hugo Luiz to the Council in 2020 with just 3 of 9 seats (an isolated minority) came to control 5 of 9 seats in 2024 (a simple majority), with a 22.7% increase in the combined vote for the ticket's candidates. That suggests a coattail effect between the majoritarian and proportional races, though this analysis doesn't isolate causality between the two.
